1 5.3.2 | The inner lining of the human trachea consists of ciliated pseudostratified columnar epithelium accompanied by mucus-secreting goblet cells. The goblet cells produce sticky mucus that traps inhaled dust particles, microorganisms, and foreign matter, while the rhythmic beating of cilia sweeps the mucus upward toward the pharynx to be swallowed or expectorated, effectively filtering the inhaled air. | Gas exchange occurs across the thin single-layered squamous epithelium of the alveoli and pulmonary capillaries in the lungs, not across the ciliated tracheal lining. | The coordinated action of ciliated epithelial cells and mucus-secreting cells traps and sweeps airborne particles out of the respiratory tract, filtering the inhaled air. | Prevention of food entering the larynx and trachea during swallowing is accomplished by the epiglottis closing over the glottis, not by ciliated tracheal cells. | Prevention of the collapse of tracheal walls is provided by C-shaped rings of hyaline cartilage embedded in the tracheal wall, not by mucosal epithelial cells.
2 5.3.9 | Inspired air contains approximately 21.0% oxygen, whereas expired air contains about 16.4% oxygen. This reduction of ~4.6% occurs because oxygen diffuses across the alveoli into the blood, binds to haemoglobin, and is transported to body cells, where it is consumed as the terminal electron acceptor in aerobic cellular respiration to generate ATP (energy production). | Lung tissues do not store oxygen; oxygen continuously diffuses into the pulmonary capillary blood down its partial pressure gradient. | Oxygen is chemically reactive and readily binds reversibly to haemoglobin in red blood cells to form oxyhaemoglobin. | Oxygen is a metabolic reactant consumed during aerobic respiration, not a metabolic waste product (carbon dioxide is the waste product). | Oxygen consumed in cellular respiration during aerobic ATP synthesis accounts for the decreased oxygen percentage in expired air.
3 5.4.1 | Asthma is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the respiratory airways characterized by hyper-responsiveness of the bronchi and bronchioles to allergens or irritants. This leads to bronchospasm (contraction and constriction of smooth muscles in bronchial walls), mucosal swelling, and excessive mucus secretion, severely narrowing the airways and causing shortness of breath (dyspnoea) and wheezing. | Bronchoconstriction and inflammation of the bronchi and bronchioles narrow the lumen, causing acute shortness of breath and wheezing in asthma. | Rupturing and breakdown of alveolar walls is the hallmark of emphysema, not asthma. | Bronchial walls do not rupture in asthma; they become inflamed, spasmed, and narrowed. | Accumulation of pus and fluid in alveoli is characteristic of pneumonia, not asthma.
4 12.4.5 | The first step in urine formation is glomerular filtration (ultrafiltration), which takes place in the renal corpuscle. The diameter of the afferent arteriole is wider than that of the efferent arteriole, creating high hydrostatic blood pressure (~55 mm Hg) in the glomerular capillaries. This hydrostatic pressure forces water and small dissolved solutes through the filtration membrane into the lumen of Bowman's capsule to form glomerular filtrate. | Blood pressure drives fluid out of capillaries rather than maintaining salts selectively in the filtrate. | High hydrostatic blood pressure within glomerular capillaries is the primary driving force for ultrafiltration to form glomerular filtrate. | Tubular secretion of hydrogen ions occurs through active transport in the renal tubules, not directly powered by glomerular blood pressure. | Glucose reabsorption occurs in the proximal convoluted tubule via active cotransport, not in the Loop of Henle.
5 12.5.3 | Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL) is a non-invasive medical procedure that uses focused high-energy acoustic shock waves (sound waves) directed from outside the body to pulverize large renal calculi (kidney stones) into sand-like particles, which can then pass painlessly through the urinary tract in urine. | X-rays (fluoroscopy) are ionizing electromagnetic radiation used only for imaging and targeting the stones, not for breaking them. | Microwaves are electromagnetic waves used in thermal ablation or communication, not in renal lithotripsy. | High-energy acoustic shock waves (ultrasonic sound waves) generate focused mechanical stresses that fragment kidney stones into passable sand. | Ultraviolet rays are non-ionizing optical radiation used for surface sterilization, having no mechanical effect on internal calculi.
6 13.2.1 | Nervous coordination is mediated by electrical impulses propagating rapidly along nerve axons (up to 100 m/s), resulting in immediate responses. Chemical coordination by endocrine hormones takes longer because hormones are secreted into interstitial fluid, enter the circulatory bloodstream, travel throughout the systemic circulation to target tissues, and bind to receptors, a transport process governed by blood flow velocity. | Both hormones and neurotransmitters have high receptor specificity; specificity does not explain the slower transmission speed. | Hormones must travel via the bloodstream throughout the circulatory system to reach distant target organs, which takes seconds to minutes compared to millisecond electrical impulses. | Hormones being potent at minute concentrations is an efficiency feature, not the physical cause of transport delay. | Small molecular size would facilitate diffusion rather than slow down response time.

9 14.1.3 | The human skeleton is divided into the axial skeleton (skull, auditory ossicles, hyoid bone, vertebral column, ribs, and sternum) and the appendicular skeleton (pectoral girdle, upper limbs, pelvic girdle, and lower limbs). The femur (thigh bone) is the major weight-bearing bone of the lower limb and is part of the appendicular skeleton. | The femur is the bone of the thigh belonging to the lower limb of the appendicular skeleton. | The stapes is a tiny auditory ossicle located in the middle ear, belonging to the axial skeleton. | The sternum (breastbone) is a flat bone situated in the anterior midline of the thorax, belonging to the axial skeleton. | The hyoid bone is a U-shaped bone in the anterior neck supporting the tongue, belonging to the axial skeleton.
10 14.3.3 | The biceps brachii and triceps brachii act as an antagonistic muscle pair around the elbow hinge joint. During flexion (bending the arm at the elbow), the biceps contracts (agonist/flexor) while the triceps relaxes. During extension (straightening the arm), the triceps contracts (agonist/extensor) while the biceps relaxes. | The biceps must contract to produce flexion force; it does not remain relaxed. | The biceps acts as a flexor by contracting during flexion and relaxing during extension. | This represents the state of the extensor muscle (triceps), not the biceps. | Antagonistic muscle coordination requires reciprocal relaxation rather than simultaneous contraction.
11 14.4.2 | Osteoporosis is characterized by decreased bone mineral density and deterioration of bone microarchitecture. In elderly post-menopausal women, cessation of ovarian function leads to a precipitous decline in oestrogen levels. Because oestrogen normally inhibits osteoclast-mediated bone resorption, its deficiency accelerates bone loss, making bones porous, fragile, and prone to fractures. | Thyroxin regulates overall basal metabolic rate, and its deficiency causes hypothyroidism, not characteristic post-menopausal osteoporosis. | Oxytocin stimulates uterine contractions during labour and milk ejection during lactation, having no primary role in post-menopausal bone loss. | Post-menopausal decline in oestrogen removes inhibition on osteoclasts, leading to accelerated bone resorption and osteoporosis. | Calcitonin deficiency is not the primary cause of post-menopausal osteoporosis; the key trigger is the loss of ovarian oestrogen.
12 15.1.2 | Asexual reproduction involves a single parent organism dividing by mitosis or fission without meiosis, gamete formation, or cross-fertilization. Consequently, all offspring are genetically identical clones of the parent and of each other, lacking genetic variation (except for rare spontaneous mutations). | The number of offspring produced does not determine genetic diversity; many asexual organisms produce millions of identical clones. | Asexual reproduction relies on mitotic division without gametic fusion or crossing over, producing genetically uniform clones lacking variation. | Many unicellular organisms reproduce sexually (e.g. via conjugation) and exhibit significant genetic variation. | Organisms reproducing asexually may possess multiple diploid or polyploid chromosomes; chromosome count does not cause lack of variation.
13 15.5.5 | In mammalian spermatogenesis, diploid primordial germ cells in the seminiferous tubules differentiate into spermatogonia. A spermatogonium divides mitotically to maintain the stem cell pool and yield daughter cells that enlarge and mature into diploid (2n) primary spermatocytes. These primary spermatocytes then undergo Meiosis-I to form haploid secondary spermatocytes, which undergo Meiosis-II to form spermatids, which mature into spermatozoa. | Mature sperms (spermatozoa) are formed at the end of spermiogenesis after both meiotic divisions, not directly from mitotic division of a spermatogonium. | Spermatids are haploid products formed after Meiosis-II of secondary spermatocytes. | Spermatogonia divide mitotically to produce diploid primary spermatocytes destined to enter meiosis. | Secondary spermatocytes are haploid cells produced after the first meiotic division of primary spermatocytes.
14 15.6.1 | Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) is a retrovirus that selectively targets and infects cells displaying CD4 surface receptors, primarily CD4+ T-helper lymphocytes (a critical type of white blood cell). Progressive viral replication and destruction of CD4+ T-cells lead to profound lymphopenia and immunodeficiency (AIDS), leaving the patient susceptible to opportunistic infections and malignancies. | HIV infection does not directly alter or increase systemic blood pH, which is tightly buffered between 7.35 and 7.45. | HIV infects and lyses CD4+ T helper white blood cells, progressively decreasing their count and crippling cell-mediated immunity. | HIV causes cell death and lysis of infected lymphocytes rather than inducing uncontrolled cellular proliferation. | Decreased oxygen carrying capacity of blood is a consequence of anaemia or carbon monoxide poisoning, not the hallmark pathology of HIV.
15 16.2.4 | Homologous chromosomes are chromosome pairs of the same length, centromere position, and gene loci. At a specific locus corresponding to gene P (e.g. for skin colour) on chromosome I, the homologous locus on chromosome II must carry an allele of that same gene—either the dominant allele (P) or the recessive allele (p). It cannot carry an allele of an entirely different, non-allelic gene like Q. | Homologous chromosome loci carry different or identical alleles of the exact same gene, so the homologous locus for gene P can carry allele P or p. | Q is a different gene locus entirely and cannot be an allele at the locus for gene P. | The individual may be heterozygous (Pp), so the allele can be either P or p, not strictly P only. | Q is an allele of gene Q located at a distinct locus, not an alternative allele for gene P.
16 16.4.7 | In Mendel's pea experiments, round seed shape is dominant (R) over wrinkled (r). The parental generation consists of two round seeds whose cross yields F1 round seed I. When F1 seed I is selfed/crossed, the F2 generation yields both round and wrinkled seeds (including seed II). For wrinkled seeds (rr) to appear in F2, F1 seed I must be heterozygous (Rr). Furthermore, for seed II (a round seed in F2) to produce both types or show heterozygosity as denoted in standard Mendelian monohybrid pedigrees, both I and II represent heterozygous (Rr) genotypes. | If I were homozygous (RR), all F2 progeny would be round (RR or Rr) and no wrinkled seeds could appear. | F1 seed I must carry recessive allele r to produce wrinkled F2 offspring, making I heterozygous (Rr). | Seed I is heterozygous and seed II in this cross structure is heterozygous (Rr). | Both seed I (F1 generation) and seed II (heterozygous F2 carrier) possess heterozygous (Rr) genotypes.

18 16.5.8 | Artificial selection (selective breeding) is the intentional breeding of plants or animals by humans to propagate specific desirable phenotypic traits. Breeding grapevine varieties over generations to produce larger fruit/berries is a classic agricultural example of artificial selection. | Selective breeding of grapevines by humans to select for larger berry size is an example of artificial selection. | Using ladybirds to prey on aphids is biological pest control, not selective breeding. | Development of antibiotic resistance in bacterial populations under drug selection pressure is natural selection. | Inserting a human insulin gene into a bacterial plasmid is recombinant DNA technology (genetic engineering), not selective breeding.
19 17.1.2 | In an ecosystem, saprotrophic fungi act as decomposers. They secrete extracellular digestive enzymes to break down complex organic compounds from dead plants, animal carcasses, and organic wastes into simple inorganic minerals (such as nitrates, phosphates, and carbon dioxide), recycling vital nutrients back into the soil and atmosphere for re-absorption by autotrophic producers. | Fungi are heterotrophs that consume oxygen during aerobic respiration; they do not produce or release oxygen. | Saprophytic fungi decompose dead organic biomass and release recycled mineral nutrients back into the ecosystem. | Adding trophic levels or interactions may occur, but the fundamental ecological function of fungi is nutrient recycling. | Fungi do not function to decrease competitive interactions among animal consumers.
20 17.2.6 | In a terrestrial ecosystem, the pyramid of biomass is upright. Energy and biomass are lost at each successive trophic step due to cellular respiration, metabolic heat loss, and unconsumed or undigested materials (~90% loss per trophic level). Consequently, autotrophic primary producers (grass) at the base of the food chain support all higher trophic levels and contain the greatest total dry biomass. | Rats are secondary consumers at the third trophic level and possess significantly less biomass than the producer grass. | Owls occupy the apex consumer trophic level and contain the smallest total biomass in the food chain. | Autotrophic producers (grass) capture solar energy and contain the greatest total biomass in an upright terrestrial pyramid of biomass. | Grasshoppers are primary consumers and have less biomass than the producers they feed on.
21 17.4.2 | Commensalism is a symbiotic relationship between two organisms of different species wherein one organism benefits while the other is neither helped nor harmed (+/0). Sessile barnacles benefit by gaining mobility, water current flow, and nutrient-rich feeding areas, while the whale is unaffected. | Predation is an interaction where a predator kills and feeds on prey (+/-), which is not the case between barnacles and whales. | Parasitism involves a parasite living on or inside a host and causing harm or deriving nourishment at the host's expense (+/-). | Mutualism is an obligate or facultative interaction where both interacting species derive mutual benefits (+/+). | Barnacles derive locomotion and feeding benefits while causing no harm or benefit to the whale, defining commensalism.
22 18.3.4 | Interferons (IFNs) are a group of signaling cytokines (glycoproteins) synthesized and released by host cells in response to viral invasion. Interferons bind to receptors on neighbouring uninfected cells, activating intracellular signaling pathways that trigger the synthesis of antiviral proteins, inhibiting viral replication, transcription, and translation, thereby limiting the spread of viral infections. | Analgesics (such as endorphins or NSAIDs) reduce pain, not antiviral interferons. | Thalassemia is a genetic haemoglobin disorder treated by blood transfusions or bone marrow transplantation, not by interferons. | Thrombolytic agents (such as tissue plasminogen activator, tPA, or streptokinase) dissolve blood clots, not interferons. | Interferons are cytokine glycoproteins secreted by virus-infected cells that stimulate surrounding cells to resist viral replication.

24 19.1.4 | Medicinal drugs (pharmaceuticals) are therapeutic chemical substances administered to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent disease, or relieve pain. Anaesthetics (used to induce reversible loss of sensation during surgery) and analgesics (painkillers used to alleviate pain) are both recognized classes of medicinal drugs. Heroin is an illicit narcotic/addictive drug with no accepted medical use, and caffeine is a dietary central nervous system stimulant. | Heroin is an illicit, highly addictive street opiate, not a clinical medicinal drug. | Caffeine is a common social dietary stimulant rather than a standard prescription medicinal drug. | Caffeine is an addictive dietary stimulant, whereas anaesthetics are clinical prescription drugs. | Both anaesthetics (sensation loss agents) and analgesics (pain relievers) are legitimate classes of medicinal pharmaceuticals.
25 19.3.2 | Antibiotics are classified by their mode of action into bactericidal and bacteriostatic agents. Bactericidal antibiotics kill bacteria directly (e.g. by disrupting cell wall synthesis). Bacteriostatic antibiotics (e.g. tetracyclines, erythromycin) inhibit bacterial cellular growth and reproduction (such as by inhibiting protein synthesis or folic acid synthesis), holding the microbial population in stationary phase so that the host's immune system can eliminate the pathogen. | Directly killing bacteria is the action of bactericidal antibiotics (such as penicillins). | Bacteriostatic antibiotics prevent bacterial growth, division, and metabolic replication without directly lysing the cells. | Developing antibiotic resistance is an adverse evolutionary adaptation of bacteria, not the therapeutic mechanism of antibiotics. | Producing active immunity against pathogens is the specific role of vaccines, not antibiotics.
